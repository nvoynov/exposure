A Journey to Svalovichi

Every journey assumes a destination and a plan. But the road has its own terms. It doesn’t care about your expectations.

One moment the car sinks into a massive puddle, and you’re looking for a tractor. The next, a local brings you wild honey. The real journey begins where you are fully involved. Where you are no longer just a passenger, occasionally glancing out the window.

On the road, the clock breaks. Linear time retreats, giving way to a simple cycle: action, contemplation, the space between… Who fills it, and with what? We just drive until evening, looking for a place to spend the night.

The companions’ expectations shatter against reality. Group dynamics: one minute singing, the next arguing. The road melts everyone down.

Wild places demand questions. The locals watch with curiosity: what are you trying to find in our backcountry? Welcoming to the point of awkwardness. Ready to give their last loaf of bread.

You feel ashamed—it never crossed our minds to bring gifts from the capital. Except maybe some vodka from a village shop. Tomorrow there will be another shop—we will restock. We will leave. They will stay.

Vanished roads. Crooked fences. Abandoned houses, once proud of their geometry, carved shutters, and porches. Why does this decay hypnotize the eye so much? Why does every ruined house feel different?

What is this? The idea of transience, where humans stepped back and nature simply reclaimed its own? Evidence of an industrial revolution that forced us to renounce the land? Or a tectonic shift in the very nature of man?

The journey continues.

“…travel on until you come to a rustic people who know nothing of the sea, a people who do not eat salt with their food… And I will give you a sign that cannot escape you: when another traveler happens to meet you and says that you have a winnowing fan on your shoulder, then fix your oar in the earth and make your sacrifice…”

— Homer, “The Odyssey”, Book 11
