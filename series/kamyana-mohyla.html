Kamyana Mohyla

Some places refuse to let you go. They call you back, though the reason remains hazy. You return time and again, trying to crack the code. Stonetomb doesn’t surrender easily. It is a monolithic labyrinth of porous, fragile sandstone—the frozen layers of the Sarmatian stratum, raised from the bottom of an ancient, long-vanished sea. The rock absorbs moisture, holds warmth, and grows patterned with lichens and moss, yet remains deaf to sudden attempts at capture. The site keeps up its defense, demanding time, silence, and just the right atmospheric state to finally expose the hidden aspects of this eternal geometry.

Once, I was fortunate enough to wander through its monolithic maze during a dense, heavy mist. In this landscape, human presence is fleeting; rare visitors appear on the periphery, only to dissolve instantly back into the gray stone, leaving behind nothing but disembodied voices that quietly vanish into the air. In those muted moments, the boundary between eras blurs, and you can almost hear the rhythmic breath of eternity.

As for the historical reality of this place, I know very little—I have heard fragments of its archaeological past, but chose not to remember a single word. I prefer the clean state of observation. Across thousands of years, a forgotten prehistoric steppe civilization left its marks here: I see ancient stone effigies (babas) guarding the horizon, and hand-cut millstones resting in the grass—proof that they once harvested grain and lived by the soil.

They document a complex, demanding site filled with unanswered questions. It is a lingering ache that, were it not for the war, I would have returned here at least a dozen times before publishing this series in 2026. Given the chance, I will undoubtedly go back.
